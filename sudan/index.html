A United Nations report published Nov 17, issued in Geneva and posted on the UN in Sudan website, said that in just over two weeks between Oct 20 and Nov 4, 29 drone strikes, attributed to the Rapid Support Forces, hit electricity facilities and fuel stations across 11 states, triggering widespread power blackouts and further fuel shortages affecting millions far from the conflict frontlines. It said the strikes had a devastating impact on civilians and essential services, causing power outages that disrupted health services including dialysis and surgical procedures, access to clean water, and food production. AFP, in a Nov 17 report on the same OHCHR findings, likewise said the intensification of drone strikes targeting power stations and fuel supplies had become a worrying trend, and quoted the report as saying that between Oct 20 and Nov 4 some 29 drone strikes attributed to the RSF hit electricity facilities and fuel stations, triggering widespread power blackouts and further fuel shortages affecting millions far from the conflict frontlines. That official humanitarian account is later than an Al Jazeera Nov 15 feature, which said the RSF has intensified attacks on power stations around Khartoum state over the past month, creating an energy crisis in the capital, and that there is no clear schedule for the resulting power cuts, which can last anywhere between six hours and a day. Al Jazeera said that between Oct 20 and Nov 2, 2026, there were at least 18 attacks on electricity infrastructure across at least nine states, leading to declining power generation capacity and fuel shortages. Citing The Associated Press, it said repeated drone attacks on power stations have seen electricity generation fall from about 4,400 megawatts before the war to about 1,100 MW today. Residents and a small-business owner in Port Sudan described prolonged, unreliable cuts, including outages lasting more than 24 hours. The UN resident and humanitarian coordinator, Denise Brown, was quoted warning that repeated strikes on power and fuel stations were disrupting dialysis, hospital births, and operations.
